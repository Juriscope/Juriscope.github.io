Balancing Accountability and Rehabilitation in Juvenile Cases

Kristyn Gibney, Grade 10 | Law

Back to research

Imagine two seventeen-year-olds. One impulsively steals a car after being pressured by friends. The other spends months planning the murder of a peer, researches how to leave no tracks, and goes through with the crime as intended. Legally, both are juveniles, but should they be treated the same? This ethical question remains one of the most difficult in criminal justice. Juvenile courts were established under the belief that children have a greater capacity for change than adults and should therefore receive rehabilitation rather than punishment. Contemporary neuroscience greatly supports this idea, demonstrating that parts of the brain responsible for impulse control, risk assessment, and long-term planning continue to develop into early adulthood. However, some crimes appear so deliberate and violent that limiting consequences based solely on age raises ethical concerns. If justice exists to protect society, honor victims’ suffering, and hold those accountable for their actions, then age alone cannot always determine responsibility. While rehabilitation should remain the focus of juvenile justice, in certain situations, trying juveniles as adults is ethically justified because it better fulfills the principles of justice, public safety and accountability.

Critics of adult prosecution point to the primary ethical justification of neuroscience. The prefrontal cortex, a part of the brain behind your forehead helping with decision making, self-control and risk assessment, continues to develop into early adulthood (Cleveland Clinic, 2025). Therefore, teenagers are more likely to make reckless decisions, underestimate consequences and submit to peer pressure. These developmental differences are the reason juvenile courts were created. Rather than emphasizing punishment, the juvenile justice system focuses on education, treatment, and rehabilitation because young people often possess a greater capacity to change than adults. Society benefits more when juveniles who make poor decisions are given opportunities to become responsible citizens rather than allowing adolescent mistakes to permanently linger with them. Also, sending these individuals directly into adult prisons can expose them to a rough environment that may reduce their educational opportunities and increase the likelihood of a repeat offense. If an individual displays a clear chance of reducing future crime through rehabilitation, ignoring that opportunity would be unethical.

These developmental differences were recognized by the United States Supreme Court in Roper v Simmons (2005). Christopher Simmons was seventeen when he planned and carried out the murder of Shirley Crook. The Court ruled it unconstitutional to impose the death penalty on offenders who committed crimes while under the age of eighteen because they have diminished culpability and a higher capacity for rehabilitation. The Court did not erase Simmons’s responsibility for the murder; instead, it recognized that his age reduced his culpability and prohibited the death penalty because he was under eighteen when he committed the crime. The decision highlights that youth affects blameworthiness without eliminating it. This distinction is vital because it suggests that age should mitigate punishment rather than erase accountability.

Acknowledging that juveniles deserve special consideration is one step, but it does not answer every ethical question. Crime exists on a spectrum, some displaying a level of planning and intent that conflicts with the idea that rehabilitation alone is an appropriate response. Courts already distinguish levels of intent when sentencing adults, so this factor should apply to juveniles too. When a teenager purposefully commits murder after months of preparation, society must consider not only the offender’s future but also the impacts on victims and overall safety of the community. Justice is not only about helping offenders become productive members of society, but is also about recognizing the seriousness of the harm inflicted on others.

This perspective is reflected in the ethical philosophy of Immanuel Kant, who argued that moral responsibility depends on an individual’s ability to make rational choices and accept the consequences of those choices (Rohlf, 2024 and Jankowiak, 2025). While adolescents generally have less mature judgement than adults, older teenagers typically understand that crimes such as murder, rape, or armed robbery are morally wrong. If courts refused to hold offenders accountable solely because they were minors, they would be breaking their moral responsibility. However, Kant’s philosophy does not mean ignoring an offender’s unique situation. Rather, it is a reminder that accountability remains an essential part of justice, even when other factors like age deserve consideration.

The importance of balancing responsibility with developmental differences can also be seen in the case Miller v. Alabama (2012). In this case, the Supreme Court ruled that mandatory life-without-parole sentences for juveniles violated the Eighth Amendment because they prevented judges from considering youth and its accompanying circumstances before giving the harshest punishments. Rather than prohibiting severe punishment completely, the Court emphasized individualized sentencing. Ethically, this approach rejects two extremes: every juvenile deserves adult punishment or that every juvenile deserves juvenile treatment. Instead, it requires courts to consider developmental factors before determining an appropriate sentence.

Another way to evaluate this issue is through utilitarianism, a theory popularized by John Stuart Mill. Under this idea, the most ethical criminal justice system is one that produces the greatest overall societal benefit while minimizing future harm. From this perspective, rehabilitation is often preferable because successfully reforming juvenile offenders reduces future crime, strengthens communities, and gives them a chance to contribute positively to society. However, it also recognizes society’s duty to protect innocent people. If a juvenile has repeatedly committed violent offenses or poses an ongoing threat, transferring the case to adult court may produce greater overall safety by preventing future victims. The ethical dilemma then extends to consequences: which response is most likely to reduce future harm while preserving justice?

Critics argue that trying juveniles as adults fails this utilitarian test. Studies have found that children in an adult jail are 36 times more likely to die by suicide than those in juvenile detention facilities (Equal Justice Initiative, 2019). They also are at a greater risk of sexual and physical violence, worse mental health outcomes, and greater rates of reoffending. In addition, racial and economic disparities have influenced which juveniles are transferred to adult court; more than half of the children under 14 transferred each year are African American or Latino (Equal Justice Initiative, 2019). These criticisms are important because a justice system cannot be ethical if it produces preventable harm or treats individuals in similar situations unequally.

However, these concerns do not prove that adult prosecution is unethical. Instead, they expose the shortcomings of grouping all juvenile cases together. Automatically transferring every juvenile accused of a violent crime to an adult court ignores developmental science and increases the risk of unjust outcomes. On the other hand, automatically preventing adult prosecution because of age alone overlooks the effects on victims and seriousness of intentional violence. Ethical decision making requires rejecting both extremes in favor of individualized judgement.

This balanced perspective is reinforced by Aristotle’s virtue ethics. Rather than focusing solely on rules, Aristotle emphasized the importance of developing virtues such as justice, compassion and fairness. A virtuous judge would use practical wisdom to determine the appropriate response in each individual case, avoiding both excessive punishment and excessive leniency. The most ethical response is therefore one that considers all circumstances of each juvenile offender, allowing accountability and rehabilitation to be pursued together.

For this reason, decisions about transferring juveniles to adult court should be made on a case by case basis. Judges should consider factors such as the offender’s age, level of maturity, degree of planning, mental health, prior criminal history, and potential for rehabilitation. This approach recognizes that no single characteristic can fully determine moral responsibility. Justice requires examining the person alongside the crime. Ultimately, the debate over juvenile offenders is a debate about what justice is itself. A society committed to punishment risks abandoning compassion, while a society committed to rehabilitation risks abandoning accountability. Neither extreme adequately reflects the complexity of juvenile crimes. By allowing age to influence, but not define, criminal responsibility allows courts to better balance individual redemption with the protection of society. In doing so, the juvenile system confirms the ethical truth that every child deserves to be seen as capable of change but every victim deserves to know that serious wrongdoing carries meaningful consequences.
